Scenario & Research Design

Scenario: A user responds to a morning smartphone alarm by selecting Snooze or Stop. Innovation: A familiar alarm interface replaces abstract pointing targets while button width and center-to-center distance are systematically varied. Application: Study how alarm control placement and size affect pointing speed and errors. This desktop-sized test arena is a controlled simulation, not a claim about real sleepy users or physical smartphone touch ergonomics.

Protocol: 3 widths (40, 60, 80 px) × 4 distances (120, 200, 280, 360 px) × 5 repetitions = 60 successful trials. Each control is requested 30 times overall. Press Begin once and click the START pad once. Thereafter, each correct click immediately presents the next target; no repeated START clicks are needed. Each target is placed exactly A pixels from the previous correct target center. The timer for each trial starts at the previous correct click (or initial START click) and ends at the next correct click. This continuous protocol includes target recognition time, so MT is an operational pointing-and-selection time rather than isolated motor movement time. Incorrect selections are counted as errors and do not end the trial. Do not resize or zoom the page during a session. Step 4 · Report

Scenario

This experiment simulates a morning alarm interface in which users respond by selecting Snooze or Stop. The research question is how the distance and size of a specified alarm control relate to the time needed to select it.

Innovation

Rather than using isolated geometric targets, the experiment keeps two recognizable alarm controls visible while systematically varying the target distance (A) and width (W). Each trial specifies the intended control, and the other control acts as a distractor. This balances a familiar interface with a repeatable pointing task.

Application and Motivation

Alarm controls are used during time-sensitive everyday interactions. Understanding the effects of target size and placement may inform more accessible interface layouts. The current study uses a mouse in a desktop browser, so its results should not be interpreted as direct measurements of finger-touch performance or waking-up behavior.

Discussion and Limitations

This experiment uses two simultaneously visible alarm controls, Snooze and Stop, to reflect a familiar everyday interface. Unlike a conventional single-target pointing task, users must identify the instructed target among two choices. Therefore, the recorded trial time may include visual attention, target identification, and decision-making in addition to pointing movement and clicking.

These cognitive components may increase the measured time and its variability, potentially affecting the fitted intercept, slope, and R². A lower R² is possible but not inevitable; the effect depends on how consistently these additional processes occur across trials. Incorrect selections are counted and their elapsed time remains in the trial duration.

Mouse input and finger touch may differ in pointing accuracy, posture, occlusion, and motor behavior. This is a desktop-browser simulation, not a test of actual waking-up behavior or smartphone touch performance. Future studies could compare single-target and dual-button conditions, or separate reaction time from movement time, to better isolate the contribution of motor control.
